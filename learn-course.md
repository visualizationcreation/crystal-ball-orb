## A prediction you can learn from

Imagine holding a crystal ball with a small, unusual inscription: show your working. Its value would come from making your uncertainty visible. Welcome to Thinking in Probabilities. We will use eight forecasts from the Crystal Ball Orb to learn how a prediction earns its place, how evidence can change it, and how to judge it fairly afterward. Our case study preserves the outlook recorded on September eighth, twenty twenty-six, for September ninth through the twenty-second. The outcomes are guesses from that dated edition, not reports of what subsequently happened.

Picture three layers inside one sphere. At the top sit broad themes, such as price pressure and policy patience. Across the middle sit specific claims about what will happen. Below them sit observations, assumptions, and unanswered questions. A theme helps you decide where to look. Evidence helps you decide what to believe. Neither can replace the other. Patience, for example, can explain why a central bank might wait. It does not tell you whether waiting will actually win the vote.

This distinction matters because a plausible story can support several endings. Shared power might produce a coalition, prolonged negotiation, or a fragile agreement. If a forecast can be declared correct under every ending, it teaches us very little. We therefore need a claim with edges: a particular outcome, a date, a source that can settle it, and a rule for ambiguous cases. These rules are like the markings on a measuring instrument. We put them there before seeing the result.

We also need a starting belief. Forecasters often call that a prior: what seems plausible before the next piece of evidence arrives. Past sporting dominance can supply one. So can a previous policy vote. A prior is useful precisely because it can be revised. Throughout this course, the displayed percentages remain the original author's subjective estimates. They are not measured market odds or the output of a calibrated statistical model. We will examine those estimates rather than borrowing certainty from their numerical appearance.

## Europe: a story needs a mechanism

Our first test arrives in Europe. The orb gives a fifty-five percent chance to the European Central Bank increasing its deposit rate by a quarter of a percentage point on September tenth. Fifty-five percent is a narrow lean. The strongest alternative, in this case, is a hold. The exact size matters: a half-point increase would not satisfy a quarter-point forecast. We are predicting one defined decision, rather than the general feeling that the central bank will sound concerned about inflation.

Why lean toward an increase? The cited August flash estimate put euro-area annual inflation at three point three percent, after two point nine percent in July. That observation helps the hike story, but it leaves a gap. An energy shock can raise prices while reducing the money households have available for other purchases. One force pushes measured inflation up; another can weaken demand. To understand the policy choice, we need to ask how far the shock spreads, how long it lasts, and what the bank believes interest rates can usefully do.

This brings us to the first knowledge floor. The July policy statement monitored indirect and second-round effects, but our orb contains no independent estimate of their size. That is a limit of this analysis. It does not mean the issue is unknowable. A useful next investigation would separate a direct energy-price increase from later changes in wages and other prices. The lesson is simple: when a compelling headline enters your forecast, identify the missing mechanism between the headline and your predicted outcome.

## America: define the number before guessing it

That same discipline becomes especially valuable when the outcome is a number. The next forecast assigns sixty percent to August United States core consumer prices rising by either zero point two or zero point three percent over the previous month, after seasonal adjustment. The September eleventh release is the event. August is the period measured. Confusing those dates would change the question. Core, in this measure, excludes food and energy directly. It is also different from the personal consumption expenditures price measure used for the Federal Reserve's inflation target.

The previous reported monthly core increase was zero point two percent. That gives us an anchor, but one observation cannot establish a reliable trend. Think of hearing one beat through a wall and trying to infer the whole rhythm. The forecast range admits some movement around that anchor. Its scoring rule is equally specific: use the rounded figure in the initial release. Later revisions are important for understanding the economy, but quietly substituting them would change the original forecasting test.

The lower layer identifies what this estimate lacks: a bottom-up assessment of the components. Rent, vehicles, airfares, and medical prices can move differently. Without estimating their contributions, the numerical prediction remains a fairly simple judgment of persistence. That does not make it useless. It tells us how much weight to give it. When you see a precise forecast, ask whether the analysis has comparable precision. A clearly admitted gap is more informative than an extra decimal place that the evidence cannot justify.

## Sweden: choose the claim you can support

Now change the kind of uncertainty. Sweden's September thirteenth election produces a much stronger call: a ninety-five percent chance that no single party wins an outright parliamentary majority. This is not a forecast of the winning party, the winning bloc, or the eventual government. Those are different questions. A good forecaster can have high confidence in one and little confidence in the others. Learning to separate them often improves judgment more than trying to make one grand prediction about an entire election.

The historical anchor is the official twenty twenty-two result. The largest party, the Social Democrats, won one hundred and seven seats out of three hundred and forty-nine. A single-party majority requires at least one hundred and seventy-five. That is a substantial distance from the historical starting point. It supports the structural prediction while offering much less help with a close contest between groups of parties. The orb does not contain a verified September polling model. Its high confidence belongs to the narrowly defined majority question.

Here the knowledge floor concerns what follows from the seats. Two parties together can cross the majority threshold even when neither crosses it alone. Arithmetic can identify possible combinations, but it cannot establish willingness to govern together. The official allocation settles our narrow claim. If only preliminary figures are available at the window's close, the assessment remains provisional. Notice the restraint: we keep the useful prediction while declining to smuggle a government forecast into it. Confidence should travel only as far as the claim and evidence allow.

## The Fed: evidence can connect two forecasts

Return to the policy calendar with that distinction in mind. The Federal Reserve forecast gives fifty-five percent to a quarter-point increase in both bounds of its target range on September sixteenth. The earlier inflation forecast is relevant here because the consumer-price release arrives five days before the decision. We now have a connection between two gold points. But a connection is not a command. Even a surprising inflation reading does not mechanically instruct the committee how to vote.

In July, the Federal Reserve retained a target range of three point five to three point seven five percent. Three dissenters preferred a quarter-point increase. That is evidence of a real policy alternative within the committee, rather than a possibility invented for dramatic balance. Yet a minority is still a minority. To justify the narrow hike lean, we need an argument about what might shift the majority. The dated employment release and the forthcoming inflation report are relevant inputs, not proof that the shift will occur.

Suppose the inflation report comes in softer than our original range. We might reasonably lower the probability of a hike, while leaving room for other evidence to matter. The right procedure is to record a new estimate with its date and reason. We retain the first estimate as well. This separates learning from moving the goalposts. Afterward, the vote is observable; the claim that one particular report caused it may remain an interpretation. Good forecasting keeps that distinction even when the final story feels obvious.

## Britain: the strongest rival belongs in the model

The British forecast makes a different choice under a related pressure: a sixty percent chance that Bank Rate stays at three point seven five percent on September seventeenth. A common energy shock does not require identical decisions across countries. Different domestic conditions and different assessments can justify different responses. The educational question is not whether a single slogan explains every bank. It is whether each branch has enough evidence to connect its own starting conditions to its own predicted decision.

The July Bank of England vote was six to three for a hold, with three members favoring four percent. The prior majority is a reasonable starting point for continuity. The minority makes a quarter-point increase a serious rival. If you were developing this forecast, you would want to understand the evidence that a previous hold voter would find persuasive. Repeating the majority's old reasoning cannot tell you whether that reasoning still fits. The strongest alternative directs your attention to the observations with the greatest potential to change your mind.

Our orb explicitly lacks a complete update of the intervening British releases. That limits its sixty percent estimate. The next useful work is concrete: inspect dated wage, services-price, and labour-market evidence, then explain how those observations bear on persistent inflation. This is what a productive knowledge floor looks like. It contains an investigable question, not a vague admission that anything could happen. The forecast remains usable as an initial judgment, while its missing research gives us a clear route to improve it.

## Japan: probability belongs to a defined outcome

Japan gives us another continuity forecast: fifty-five percent that the overnight call-rate guideline remains around one percent at the September eighteenth decision. The word guideline matters. A daily traded interest rate could fluctuate without changing the policy instruction. If we watched the wrong number, we could label a correct policy forecast incorrect. Before gathering more evidence, check that your outcome variable is actually the thing you intended to predict. A beautifully reasoned answer to a different question is still the wrong answer.

The cited July statement set the guideline around one percent by an eight-to-one vote. The dissenting proposal was around one point two five percent. An eight-to-one majority offers a stronger starting signal of agreement than a closely divided vote. But it is not an eighty-nine percent probability of another hold. Vote shares describe a past decision. A forecast probability describes uncertainty about a future outcome. Converting one directly into the other would be an unsupported shortcut, especially when the economic evidence can change between meetings.

The missing update here concerns later Japanese wages, prices, and Board remarks. A stable group of decision-makers can reach a new conclusion after receiving new information. That is why the original estimate stays close to even odds despite the prior supermajority. You could disagree with that estimate and still follow a sound method: identify the data you believe matter, explain their direction, and retain a meaningful alternative. The aim is a judgment someone else can examine, rather than a confident number that conceals its assumptions.

## The Asian Games: match the evidence to the horizon

Sport changes the setting, but it exposes the same problem of matching evidence to a question. The Asian Games begin on September nineteenth. Our forecast gives eighty percent to China leading the gold-medal table after competition on September twenty-second, Japan time. That is an early table, not the final result of a Games that extends into October. The two-week boundary is part of the claim. We cannot borrow the eventual winner and pretend it answers a question about the opening days.

The Olympic Council of Asia reported that China topped the medal standings at eleven consecutive Asian Games through Hangzhou, where it won two hundred and one gold medals. This provides a substantial historical prior for competitive depth. However, a total accumulated across an entire Games is not the same measurement as a snapshot after a few days. Imagine judging a long concert by which instrument you hear first. Sequence can temporarily change the picture even when the overall pattern is strong.

The key uncertainty is therefore the order of medal events. Our initial orb has no event-by-event medal simulation. Japan or another delegation could benefit from the early schedule. The rule uses gold first, then silver, then bronze, with an exact tie counting as joint first. If no recoverable official tally exists, scoring waits. The wider lesson applies well beyond sport: historical evidence becomes less transferable when the unit, setting, or time horizon changes. Ask what changed before deciding how much of the past to carry forward.

## The UN: make a qualitative claim reproducible

The final scheduled case turns speech into something we can assess. The orb gives sixty-five percent to explicit climate references appearing in at least half of the first ten national statements on the United Nations debate's opening day, September twenty-second. This predicts agenda attention. It does not predict a treaty, financing delivered, or lower emissions. Narrowing the claim makes it possible to test while preserving the distinction between words and material action.

The official programme places the General Debate from September twenty-second through the twenty-eighth. It also lists a climate event on the twenty-third. That later event provides context for the agenda, but it falls outside our forecast window. We count the first ten completed Member State statements in official order, excluding the Secretary-General and the Assembly President. Explicit discussion of climate change, global warming, or climate finance counts, including clear equivalents in official translations. Mere prominence in a programme does not establish what national speakers will say.

Could two careful listeners reproduce the same count? That is the knowledge-floor question here. They need a small audit record: speaker, speaking order, timestamp, and the passage supporting each positive classification. They should handle ambiguous references conservatively. If fewer than ten statements finish on that day, the original rule leaves the result unresolved. We have converted an impression about diplomatic attention into an observable test, while preserving its limits. That same habit can make many apparently qualitative forecasts much more useful.

## Leave the crystal ball better than you found it

Now look back across the sphere. Several policy calls share an energy-shock theme. That means their errors may cluster. Four forecasts built on one mistaken assumption are not four independent confirmations of that assumption. Spread your attention across genuinely different evidence, and record common dependencies. Likewise, one correct fifty-five percent call proves very little about the quality of the percentage. Calibration asks what happens across many comparable predictions: do outcomes assigned roughly sixty percent occur roughly sixty times in a hundred? Eight varied cases cannot establish that track record.

You can still begin a useful learning record now. Write down the claim, the probability, the cutoff, the evidence, and what would change your mind. Later, preserve the original rule while adding the observed result. For a resolved yes-or-no prediction, one simple score is the squared distance between your probability and the outcome, with yes represented by one and no by zero. Smaller is better. This penalizes confident errors, but a small collection still needs interpretation. Above all, keep unresolved cases visible. The inscription on our imaginary crystal ball now has a second line: leave room to be corrected. You have a way to ask sharper questions, carry evidence only as far as it supports, and make each forecast a record you can learn from.
